Muammar Gaddafi's connection to "black banks" and financial initiatives centers on two major historical subjects: his 1996 pledge to fund Black-owned banks in the United States and his vision for pan-African financial institutions.

In 1996, the former Libyan leader pledged a $1 billion donation to the Nation of Islam, led by Louis Farrakhan. The proposed funds were intended to empower Black Americans and promote economic independence.

Intended Use: Insider reports at the time indicated that roughly ($400) million was earmarked to be invested in Black-, Arab-, and Hispanic-owned banks to fuel loan programs in minority communities. The Outcome: The United States government blocked the transfer. The Clinton administration and the Treasury Department intervened, stating the funds violated strict U.S. sanctions imposed on Libya as a state sponsor of terrorism.

Gaddafi was a major proponent of Pan-African economic independence and heavily advocated for the creation of continent-wide financial structures to reduce reliance on Western institutions like the World Bank and the IMF.

He championed the creation of three major continental financial bodies: the African Central Bank (headquartered in Abuja, Nigeria), the African Monetary Fund (Yaoundé, Cameroon), and the African Investment Bank (Tripoli, Libya).

He also proposed a gold-backed African currency (the Gold Dinar) to be used for trade across the continent, intending to bypass both the U.S. dollar and the French-backed CFA franc.

Direct Involvement in Major Bombings

Gaddafi's intelligence services were directly implicated in mass-casualty attacks on Western civilians:

UTA Flight 772 Bombing (1989): A French airliner was blown up over Niger, killing 170 people. A French court later convicted six Libyans in absentia for the plot.

Gaddafi used Libya's immense oil wealth to fund, weaponize, and provide safe haven to an array of militant groups worldwide, regardless of their ideology, so long as they destabilized his adversaries:

The Provisional Irish Republican Army (IRA): Libya shipped tons of plastic explosives (Semtex) and heavy weaponry to the IRA, which was used in deadly attacks across the UK.

Palestinian Militant Groups: He funded factions such as the Abu Nidal Organization and the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO).

Other Global Militants: He supported Spain's ETA separatists, Sierra Leone's Revolutionary United Front (RUF), and various anti-Western insurgencies in South America and Asia.

State-Sponsored Assassinations

Gaddafi operated a policy of "physical liquidation" against political dissidents. Libyan hit squads, known as "Revolutionary Committees," routinely assassinated exiled Libyan critics living in European capitals like London, Bonn, and Rome. He was also implicated in failed plots to assassinate moderate Arab leaders, including the presidents of Egypt and Sudan.

By the late 1990s and early 2000s, crippling international sanctions forced Gaddafi to shift his foreign policy. In 2003, his regime formally accepted responsibility for the Lockerbie bombing, paid over $1 billion in compensation to victims' families, surrendered its weapons of mass destruction (WMD) programs, and shared intelligence with the West to fight Al-Qaeda. As a result, the U.S. removed Libya from the State Sponsors of Terrorism list in 2006.
