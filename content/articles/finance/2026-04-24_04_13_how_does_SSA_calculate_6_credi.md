# Understanding Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) and State Support Programs
To qualify for Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI), the Social Security Administration (SSA) requires earning a certain number of credits within a specific timeframe. For younger workers, typically under the age of 24, this means earning 6 credits in a 3-year period.

## Earning Credits for SSDI
The SSA looks at total annual earnings from jobs covered by Social Security to determine credits. The annual maximum is 4 credits per year, and credits are based on total annual income, not months or hours worked. For 2026, one credit is earned for every $1,890 in covered earnings. This amount changes annually:

* 2026: $1,890 per credit ($7,560 for the maximum of 4 credits)
* 2025: $1,810 per credit ($7,240 for the maximum of 4 credits)
* 2024: $1,730 per credit ($6,920 for the maximum of 4 credits)

To meet the "6 credits in 3 years" requirement, these credits must be spread over at least two of the three years. For example, if you become disabled in 2026 at age 23, the SSA will check your earnings from 2023, 2024, and 2025. You would meet the requirement if you earned the maximum 4 credits in 2025 and at least 2 credits in 2024.

You can verify your current standing by checking your official Social Security Statement online.

## Interactions with State Unemployment Insurance (UI)
Receiving state Unemployment Insurance (UI) can complicate the application process for Social Security benefits because the programs have conflicting requirements. In Washington State, applying for UI after being out of work for over a year is possible but difficult due to specific work-hour requirements.

To receive UI, you must certify that you are ready, willing, and able to work, which contradicts the requirement for SSDI or SSI that you must prove you are unable to work due to a disability. While the SSA does not strictly forbid receiving both, a judge may see the UI claim as evidence that you are not truly disabled, potentially leading to a denial of benefits.

UI is considered "unearned income" for SSI, and every dollar of UI (after the first $20) usually reduces your SSI payment by one dollar. However, UI generally does not reduce SSDI benefit amounts or threaten eligibility directly because it is not counted as earned income under Social Security's Substantial Gainful Activity (SGA) rules.

## UI Eligibility in Washington State
To apply for UI in Washington, you must meet the 680-hour rule:

* Standard Base Year: You must have worked at least 680 hours in a position covered by Washington's UI program during your "base year"—the first four of the last five completed calendar quarters.
* The 18-Month Rule: You must have worked in Washington at some point in the last 18 months. If you have been out of work for more than 18 months, you likely will not have enough hours in your base year to qualify for a new claim.
* Alternate Base Year: If you don't meet the 680-hour requirement in the standard base year, the Washington Employment Security Department (ESD) can look at your "alternate base year" (the last four completed quarters), but you still must have worked 680 hours in that timeframe.

## State Support Programs for People Unable to Work
In Washington State, there are specific programs designed as "bridges" for people who cannot work due to a disability but do not qualify for traditional Unemployment Insurance (UI). These programs are often aligned with federal disability applications rather than hindering them.

### Aged, Blind, or Disabled (ABD) Cash Assistance
This program provides a monthly cash grant (up to $450 for individuals) for low-income adults who are likely to meet federal SSI disability criteria but are still waiting for approval.

### Housing and Essential Needs (HEN) Referral
If you meet the medical criteria but don't qualify for ABD cash, you may be referred to HEN for help with rent, utilities, and personal health/hygiene items.

### Paid Family and Medical Leave (PFML)
If you worked at least 820 hours in Washington within the last 12–18 months, you might qualify for up to 12 weeks of partial wage replacement while you recover from a serious health condition.

## Impact on Federal Applications (SSI/SSDI)
Unlike UI, applying for ABD or HEN actually requires you to pursue federal benefits. These programs often provide an SSI Facilitator to help you with the complex federal application process.

If you are awarded federal SSI, the state may take a cut of your retroactive back-pay to reimburse itself for the ABD cash you received. This repayment rule is currently in effect until October 2028.

Receiving state cash assistance while getting SSI may reduce your SSI payment dollar-for-dollar because the state cash is counted as earned income.
