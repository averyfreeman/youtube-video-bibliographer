## How John Paulson Shorted the Housing Market

John Paulson’s bet against the U.S. housing market is a textbook case of using credit‑default swaps (CDS) and synthetic CDOs to profit from a systemic bubble. The strategy unfolded in four distinct phases.

### 1. Identifying the Bubble
Paulson’s team dug into granular housing data rather than relying on Wall Street consensus. They observed:

- Home‑price appreciation far outpacing historical trends.
- A rapid decline in lending standards, with “teaser” adjustable‑rate mortgages (ARMs) masking true borrower risk.

When those teaser rates reset, defaults would surge, threatening the mortgage‑backed securities (MBS) built on those loans.

### 2. Using Credit Default Swaps (CDS)
Short‑selling physical homes or conventional housing stocks was impractical at scale, so Paulson turned to CDS—essentially insurance contracts on debt.

- **Mechanics:** Paulson’s fund paid periodic premiums to major banks (Goldman Sachs, Bear Stearns, Lehman Brothers).
- **Payoff:** If the targeted MBS performed, the premiums were a loss. If the mortgages defaulted, the banks were obligated to compensate Paulson’s fund for the full loss on the securities.

Thus, the CDS acted as a direct short position on the underlying mortgage assets.

### 3. Creating Synthetic CDOs
As the pool of existing high‑risk mortgages dwindled, Paulson needed a way to scale his bet. He collaborated with investment banks—most notably Goldman Sachs—to construct synthetic collateralized debt obligations (CDOs).

- The banks selected the riskiest subprime mortgages to serve as the reference pool.
- Other investors bought the synthetic CDOs, trusting credit‑rating agencies that labeled them safe.
- Behind the scenes, Paulson was effectively betting against the very instruments he helped assemble, allowing him to amplify his exposure without owning the underlying loans.

### 4. Holding Through the Pain
The trade was not an immediate windfall. Early on, Paulson’s fund lost tens of millions as housing prices continued to rise and investors withdrew capital. Nevertheless, he maintained the position.

When the subprime market collapsed in the summer of 2007, the CDS contracts surged in value, delivering an estimated **$15 billion** to Paulson’s firm and netting him personally over **$4 billion**. The trade is frequently cited as “The Greatest Trade Ever.”
