Feeding Tuna or Mackerel to Cats: Safety Considerations
===========================================================

As a cat owner, it's essential to be aware of the potential risks associated with feeding tuna or mackerel to your feline friend. These fish, being high on the food chain, accumulate high levels of mercury, which can lead to heavy metal poisoning, nutrient deficiencies, and urinary tract issues in cats over time.

Veterinarians generally recommend feeding human-grade tuna or mackerel only as an occasional treat, limited to 1-2 times a week, with portions kept to about a teaspoonful per serving. Treats like this should make up no more than 10% of your cat's total caloric intake.

High-quality commercial cat foods, such as Tiki Cat, are much safer than feeding human-grade raw or canned fish. However, even with these safer options, experts still generally recommend against a daily, fish-only diet.

Smaller Fish and Seafood Varieties: A Safer Option
------------------------------------------------

Smaller fish and seafood varieties, like shrimp and salmon, are generally considered safer options than tuna and mackerel. They sit lower on the food chain and naturally accumulate less mercury. Shrimp, for example, has a very low average mercury load of approximately 0.009 ppm, while canned salmon averages around 0.014 ppm.

Tiki Cat specifically tests its Wild Alaskan Salmon for heavy metals, including mercury, and radiation. Salmon is also a rich source of omega-3 fatty acids, which support skin and coat health, and is one of the rare natural sources of vitamin D for cats. Shrimp provides beneficial nutrients like vitamin B12 and choline.

Important Considerations for Daily Feeding
--------------------------------------------

While smaller fish and seafood varieties are safer, there are still some concerns to keep in mind:

*   Thiaminase and Iodine: Excessive fish consumption can be high in iodine, which has been linked to feline hyperthyroidism. Fish can also contain an enzyme that destroys thiamine, potentially leading to neurological issues if fed as the sole diet without proper balance.
*   Treatment of Shrimp: Shrimp is non-toxic and nutritious but is generally recommended as a treat (less than 10% of daily calories) or as part of a complete food because it does not provide all the nutrition a healthy feline needs on its own.
*   Mineral Content: Fish-heavy diets can be high in magnesium and phosphorus, which may contribute to urinary tract or kidney issues in some cats.

Recommendation
--------------

If your cat will only eat fish, rotating between salmon, shrimp, and even smaller fish like sardines or anchovies—which also have low mercury levels—is much safer than a diet of tuna and mackerel. Consider trying Tiki Cat recipes that blend these smaller fish with chicken to help balance your cat's intake. This can help minimize the risks associated with a fish-only diet and provide a more balanced and nutritious meal for your feline friend.
